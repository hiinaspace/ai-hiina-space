# How knit cuff edges and the stockinette-to-rib join are built, for yarn-level modelling

- Principal and contact: Hiina (hiina@hiina.space)

Hiina's question, as the principal posed it: a yarn-level simulation of a knitted sleeve cuff shows a visible bar where stockinette meets 2x2 rib, and the cast-on edge thread does not look like it weaves through the loops the way a real machine-knit edge does. How are these details actually constructed, so the model can be corrected? Short answers: the ridge exists in real knitting but is damped by tension and draw-in, which the simulation probably lacks. No source found describes a thread woven through the last loops of a commercial machine-knit edge except linking and sewn bind-offs. The original report was a web-only pass by earlier agent sessions. I rewrote it and did not repeat any of it. Several claims rest on search-result summaries of pages those sessions did not open, and the report marks them as such. Several constructions are the agents' inference, not found in a source. Those are labelled below.

Labels used here: `[sourced]` was read on the page; `[summary]` comes from a search summary of a page that was not opened; `[inferred]` is the agents' reasoning; `[unverified]` is from memory or not found.

## 1. Findings

- **The join bar is real knitting, but small.** Guides describe a ridge of purl bumps where rib meets stockinette. The first row of purl loops after the rib has nothing above it to push it back. Knitters hide it with a smaller rib needle, a transition row that slips the knit stitches and purls the purls, and a tighter wrap on the first purl after knits.
- **The simulated bar is plausibly the unmitigated version.** It is the first rib row's purl loops over a knit parent. Real fabric damps this with shorter loops (a rib needle two to three sizes smaller) and with draw-in, where purl columns recede behind knit columns. Shortening the rest length of that row's loops is more likely to work than another radius profile. This is the agents' judgment, not a tested result.
- **On a machine the join is probably built differently.** On a V-bed machine, rib is knitted on both beds. At the change to jersey the back-bed loops are transferred to the front bed, so no row of purl loops stands at the join. This is `[inferred]` and no source confirmed it.
- **The top-edge thread is not settled.** Candidates are a linking chain, a tubular rib start's inner layer, a folded welt's rehung row, or a sewn bind-off. A close photo of the actual cuff would decide. The report asked for one and none was available.
- **Aran backgrounds.** Moss, double moss and trinity all appear as Aran backgrounds. No source said which is most common. Double moss matches 2x2 rib columns in its first row `[inferred]`, so the join into it should be cleaner than stockinette into rib.
- **Yarn parameters.** No numeric parameter tables were found in the published yarn-level simulation papers where the agents could read them. A wool yarn-on-yarn friction coefficient was not found.

## 2. Cuff edge constructions and the yarn path each implies

Rib trims on flat machines are knitted as separate strips or tubes and linked on, or knitted in as part of a welt on a V-bed `[inferred, standard practice, not source-confirmed]`. Fully fashioned garments are assembled by cup seaming (sides, sleeves, underarms) and linking (shoulders, collars) `[summary]`. A cup seamer is advertised for joining cuffs to sleeves `[summary]`.

**a) Waste yarn and ravel cord start (open-loop edge).** `[sourced]` The machine knits several rows of waste yarn, then one row in ravel cord (smooth, strong, non-shedding), then the main yarn. Pulling out the cord later drops the waste and leaves the first main course's loops live. Each of those loops was knitted through a cord loop. After removal no thread remains in the edge, only open loops `[inferred]`. For rib starts on a V-bed the stitches are then transferred to the main bed `[summary]`. A garment that keeps the waste or cord thread is not ravelled. In a model, the thin cast-on thread through the first loops represents this construction before the cord is pulled.

**b) Tubular rib start (closed, double-layer edge).** An industrial start library `[sourced]` describes a double-bed tube. The outer layer is plain knit on alternating needles. The inner layer is sparse, on every fourth needle in the basic variant, and a "fine" variant puts it on every third needle for shorter floats and a tighter edge. The outside knits twice as long as the inside across a 6-row repeat. A hand-knit equivalent `[sourced]` works loops alternately onto the front and back of a provisional tail, then four foundation rows each knit one layer's columns and slip the other's. The result is a doubled, rounded fold. The agents' generalisation `[inferred]` is two courses of double-bed knitting: the outer layer holds the knit columns, the inner layer holds the purl columns, the yarn alternates layers each course, and the fold is where they turn. Small inner-layer loops visible from inside are one candidate for the "loop inside the cuff" that Hiina observed.

**c) Folded (hem) welt.** `[sourced]` After waste yarn and ravel cord, the hem rows are knitted at a tension one dial number lower, then a turning row two numbers looser, then the outer rows at normal tension. At the end the first main-yarn row is rehung on the needles so each live loop is knitted together with its matching new loop. A row knitted two to three numbers tighter keeps the join from bulging. The fold shows the turning row as a loose ridge on one side. The turning row, or a stitch lost at the cut yarn end, is a second candidate for the inner loop `[inferred]`.

**d) Linking and chain-stitch joins.** `[summary]` Loops of the trim edge and the garment edge are impaled together on the points of a rotary dial, and a single-thread chain stitch is formed through each pair, each chain loop pulled through the previous. Loops go on one-by-one or two-by-two. A skipped loop is not held by the seam and can run. Patents describing this: US3732832A (attaching binding strips), US3802358A (linking, two-by-two), US3340834A (knitting and linking articles). The agents' yarn path `[inferred]`: pair each last-course loop with a trim first-course loop, run a thin thread through both loops of pair i, form a chain loop, and pull it through pair i-1's chain loop. That gives a chain-stitch row on one face and a straight thread behind the loops on the other. For a single edge with no trim, the chain passes through the last-course loops alone, a linked-off edge. This is the closest match found to a thread visibly weaving through every loop `[inferred, no photo]`.

**e) Sewn tubular or Kitchener-style bind-off.** `[summary]` The Italian double-rib bind-off is a sewn bind-off where the needle follows the path the yarn would take if each stitch were knitted. The sewing thread goes through each knit loop front to back and each purl loop back to front `[inferred]`, so the edge reads as a row of loops under a thread, with knit and purl pairs visible.

**f) Hand-knit Aran practice.** A long-tail cast-on with a matching plain bind-off is the common default, and tubular cast-on is the polished option `[unverified]`. A tubular cast-on for 2x2 rib needs rearranged stitches or the Italian method `[summary]`. No source said what traditional Aran sweaters usually use.

## 3. The join between stockinette and rib

**Does a ridge exist?** Yes, as described by knitting guides. One guide `[summary]` says the purl bumps from the ribbing's last row stick out after the switch because no row of purls above them pushes them back. Another `[summary]` says the knit-to-purl change itself enlarges stitches on either side. A tech-knitting article `[sourced]` places the effect at the knit face, where the rib's knit columns run into stockinette's V's. The ridge is the horizontal line of purl bumps across the purl columns.

**Why it usually does not read as a bar** `[sourced]`: purl columns recede behind knit columns, so only a small bump shows; the rib is knitted on smaller needles; and the knit columns continue unchanged through the join. In a stretched swatch the purl columns show, and relaxed they are hidden `[summary]`.

**Fixes from guides.**
- Top-down (stockinette, then rib) `[sourced]`: with the rib needle, usually two to three sizes smaller than the body needle, work one more row of the body pattern. On the first rib row slip every knit stitch purlwise and purl every purl stitch. Then work the rib normally. This works for 1x1 and 2x2 and needs swatching for 3x3 or wider.
- Bottom-up `[sourced]`: slip the knits and purl the purls on the last rib row, then change to the larger needle. Make body-count increases on purl columns only, never on knit columns, so the V's continue.
- First purl after knits `[summary]`: wrap the yarn the other way round the needle, which uses less yarn and closes the gap, then knit that stitch through the back loop next row.
- Hem flare: a smaller needle or fewer stitches, then increase before the stockinette `[summary]`.

**Terminology.** No source called the join row a "jog". A jog is the round-join stripe effect in circular work, a different thing. Sources use "purl bumps", "ridge", "flare" and "weak spot".

**Draw-in numbers.** Sources are qualitative. Wider ribs pull in more `[sourced]`, and 1x1 is more elastic than 2x2 because it has more knit/purl transitions `[sourced]`. One study `[summary]` of 1x1 and 2x2 rib found vertical density rose about 60% from machine state to relaxed state, which is about course density and not width. No stockinette-versus-rib width ratio was found. The commonly quoted figure of relaxed rib at roughly 0.5 to 0.7 of stockinette width is from memory only `[unverified]`. Hiina's simulation reports a mean draw-in of 0.75 (0.685 at the narrowest point), so the narrowest point falls in that range and the mean sits just above it, but the range itself is unconfirmed.

## 4. The Aran background

Moss (seed), double moss and trinity are all used as Aran backgrounds in patterns `[summary]`. Which is most common on traditional sweaters was not sourced. They differ visibly: moss is a 1-stitch checker, double moss a 2-stitch checker with rows in pairs, trinity a 3-stitch cluster stitch.

A 1x1 rib into moss sets the first moss row as P1,K1 because the previous row was 1x1 `[summary]`. For 2x2 rib into double moss `[inferred]`: rows 1 and 2 are K2P2 and rows 3 and 4 are P2K2, so row 1 matches the rib's column parity and the columns swap only every two rows. Purl bumps are already everywhere in the background, which would hide a bar. That could be why the real Aran shows none. Body increases are typically made on the last rib row `[summary]`.

## 5. Yarn parameters

- **Twist.** Specific bending rigidity falls as twist rises in wool and polyester staple yarns `[summary]`. Multi-ply worsted bending can be modelled as coaxial helices `[summary]`.
- **Knitting changes the yarn.** One cotton and wool study `[summary]` found flat knitting reduced bending modulus by 3 to 30%, tensile by 2 to 10% and shear by 8 to 80%, and raised the friction coefficient by 6 to 23%. The same source says higher friction gives shorter loops in plain knit but longer loops in 1x1 rib.
- **Crimp.** No source linked fibre crimp to loop elasticity or shape. Crimp gives bulk and spring recovery in real wool `[unverified]`.
- **Published yarn-level simulations.** A 2008 paper models yarn as an inextensible flexible B-spline tube with penalty contact forces and velocity-filter friction, and its project page had no numbers `[sourced]`. A 2012 stitch-mesh paper and a 2018 paper concern building and periodic patterns, not parameters `[summary]`. A 2024 arXiv paper (2401.15169) uses a Cosserat rod yarn with stiffness taken from rough fibre tensile tests, one uniform friction coefficient (value not stated), and rest lengths shrunk by 0.8 for knits and 0.9 for wovens `[sourced]`. The agents did not identify the paper by title. A 2024 volumetric homogenisation paper is yarn-level based, but it could not be read because the file was too large.
- **What Hiina's yarn-level simulator (Cosserat rods with contact and friction) can represent** (from the builders' notes, not from reading the code): bending and stretch stiffness, twist terms, radius including per-vertex radius, friction, and rest length through the generated rest geometry. It cannot represent fibre crimp, hairiness, felting, plastic set, hysteresis, or yarn liveliness (residual twist torque), unless these are built into rest curvature or twist.

## 6. Implications for a yarn-level model

These are the agents' recommendations from the sources above. None was tested.

- **Join bar** (first rib row's purl loops standing about 2 mm proud): mostly tension and loop length, partly geometry. The topology matches real knitting, but real rib is knitted on a smaller needle and draws in. Suggested experiment order: (1) give the first rib row's loops, knit and purl columns both, a rest length around 0.8 to 0.9 of the body loop length, which is the analogue of two to three smaller needle sizes and of the 0.8 knit shrink factor in the arXiv paper, then let the simulation relax; (2) try the slip transition; (3) only then adjust the purl head shape. The existing draw-in was applied as a radius profile, which the agents expect to be the weaker lever.
- **Edge thread not visibly weaving:** a geometry and radius issue more than a yarn-parameter one. The thread must be generated through the loops with correct over-under order and clearance. The existing check, that the thread is held once with alternating signs, may already pass, so this may be a rendering problem: the builders' notes say the thin thread is drawn at full radius in the viewer and is hidden in the loops.
- **Rib elasticity and loop shape:** yarn and tension (stretch and bending stiffness, friction, loop length).
- **Slip transition as a topology change:** on the first rib row only the purl columns make a new loop and the knit columns carry the row above through. The working yarn crosses in front of the slipped knit columns between purls. The generator would need a slip-stitch element, and the first rib row would have half the loops. This removes the knit-parent purl bar in real knitting `[sourced for the technique, inferred for the model]`.

Yarn paths to generate, per construction, are those in section 2: ravel-cord start (including the open variant with the thread removed), linking chain, tubular inner layer, folded welt with rehung loops, and sewn bind-off. All are `[inferred]` and need checking against a photo.

## 7. Limits and open items

- Not opened: the 2008 yarn-simulation paper's PDF (too large to fetch), the 2024 homogenisation paper (too large), a knitting-technology textbook, and a 2020 rib paper. No numeric yarn or draw-in ranges are confirmed.
- No number for rib draw-in relative to stockinette was found.
- No photo of the machine-knit cuff was available to the agents, so the identification of the "loop inside the cuff" is a ranked guess: linking chain, tubular inner layer, or folded welt. A single continuous chain of loops would point to linking, a second layer of small loops to a tubular start, and a row of loops stitched down flat to a folded welt.
- The Aran background stitch on the real sweater was not known.
